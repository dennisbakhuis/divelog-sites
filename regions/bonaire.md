Bonaire protected its entire coastline in 1979, from the high-water line down to 60 m, all the way
round the island and round Klein Bonaire. That was decades before it was a normal thing to do, and
it is the single reason the diving is what it is. Anchoring is banned; there are fixed moorings.
Spearfishing is banned. Taking anything, living or dead, is banned. Every diver pays a nature fee
and the fee is what funds the park — STINAPA, the parks foundation, runs on it.

The practical consequence is the yellow stones. Eighty-odd dive sites are marked with a painted
yellow rock at the roadside, and you park, kit up on the tailgate and walk in. There is no boat and
no schedule. Almost every shore site on the island is one of those.

The reef profile barely varies: a shallow terrace of coral rubble and staghorn to about 8 m, a drop
-off, then a slope of hard coral, sponges and gorgonians running to 30 m and beyond. The leeward
west coast is calm nearly every day of the year, which is the other half of the explanation.

Klein Bonaire, the uninhabited islet offshore, became a legally protected reserve in 2001. The east
coast — Cai, White Hole — is exposed to the trades and only diveable on rare flat days.

The island's other history is written into the site names. **Salt Pier** is the loading jetty of
the Cargill solar salt works. **Red Slave** is at the southern tip beside the salt pans, where four
colour-coded obelisks were raised in 1837 as marks for the salt ships and rows of stone huts 1.5 m
high were built in 1850 for the enslaved people who worked the pans and walked back to Rincon at
the end of the week. Slavery was abolished in the Dutch Antilles in 1863. **Karpata** is named for
the castor-oil plant, *karpata* in Papiamentu, that grows along that stretch of coast, and sits
below the ruins of the Karpata plantation house.
