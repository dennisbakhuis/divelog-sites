Almost none of these lakes are natural. The Netherlands has been digging holes in itself for
centuries and then letting them fill, and every generation of digging left a different kind of
diving.

**Peat.** From the Golden Age onward, peat was cut for fuel and shipped to the cities. The
**Vinkeveense Plassen** are the classic case: the turf went to Amsterdam, and what was left was
shallow water divided by *legakkers*, the long narrow banks where the wet peat was laid out to dry.

**Sand for the motorways.** In the 1960s and 70s the same lakes were dug again, far deeper, for
sand — the A2 motorway and the Amsterdam-Zuidoost development both came out of Vinkeveen, which
is why a peat lake now has holes 50 m deep in it and a string of spoil islands, the
**Zandeilanden**, along its southern edge. The same thing happened all over the country:
**Bussloo**, **Slijk-Ewijk**, **De Beldert**, **Bronsbergen** and the rest are motorway and
dyke-building borrow pits that were landscaped into recreation lakes afterwards.

**River gravel.** In Limburg the Maas floodplain was worked for gravel, leaving deep, clear-ish
pits like the **Boschmolenplas** and **De Grote Hegge** at Thorn.

What they share: fresh water, a thermocline that in summer puts 20 °C over 6 °C, visibility that
depends entirely on the algae and the last person to swim past, and an economy of sunken objects —
boats, cars, aircraft, a bus, garden gnomes — put there by clubs to give people something to find.
Most Dutch dive training happens in lakes like these.
