The Lot is the best cave-diving ground in Europe and the reason is geology. The *causses* — Gramat,
Martel, Limogne — are plateaus of Jurassic limestone a few hundred metres thick. Rain falls on
them and disappears: there are almost no surface streams on the causse itself. The water travels
underground, sometimes for tens of kilometres, and comes back out at the foot of the plateau where
the limestone meets the river valleys. Those exits are the *résurgences*, and they are what people
dive.

Because the water comes out under pressure at the bottom of a cliff, the entrances are
characteristically a pool at the edge of a road or a field, with the passage going in horizontally
and down. There is no dry cave to walk through first. You park, kit up, and the dive starts at the
surface of the pool.

Four systems account for most of the diving in the region:

- **Système de l'Ouysse** — the Ouysse sinks on the Causse de Gramat and resurges below Rocamadour
  at **Cabouy** and **Saint-Sauveur**, with **Font del Truffe** in the same basin.
- **Système de Padirac** — the underground river of the Gouffre de Padirac, whose outlet at
  **Fontaine Saint-Georges** was proved by a fluorescein trace in 1947: 75 kg poured into the Lac
  des Grands Gours took 114 days to appear.
- **Réseau Gramat-Sud** — drains 330 km² of the southern Causse de Gramat into the Célé at the
  **Ressel** and **Marche-Pied**.
- **Système du Charlan** — the Charlan sink at Vidaillac, coming out at the **Trou Madame**.

Three more sites — **Landenouse**, **Cunhac** and **Combe Nègre** — are independent resurgences
with no named system behind them.

The Lot is also where the technique was worked out. French cave divers pioneered the scooter, the
staged-bottle push and the rebreather traverse here; the Ressel in particular has been a proving
ground since the 1980s and has been pushed past 4 km.
